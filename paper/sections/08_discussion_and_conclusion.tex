\subsection{Context-Growth Dynamics and Multi-Turn Token Economics}
As illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:token_sinks}, multi-turn agent execution exhibits distinctive context characteristics:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Prior History Accounts for the Majority of Context (90.5\%)}: Across all three paradigms, re-reading prior conversational turns accounts for over 90\% of cumulative input tokens. Initial prompt length optimizations (e.g., saving $\approx 6,400$ tokens in initial headers) are quickly eclipsed by the volume of accumulated dialogue and tool outputs.
    \item \textbf{On-Demand Skill Context Accumulation}: Loading procedural skill documents on-demand resulted in Superpowers accumulating \textbf{411,332,767 cache read tokens} (\texttt{cached\_content\_token\_count}), compared to 322,810,412 for Supreme and 295,918,105 for Baseline \cite{google2026gemini36}. Inserting extensive documentation into the active turn history increases context volume on subsequent turns, showing that dynamic retrieval is associated with additional accumulated context volume across subsequent turns.
    \item \textbf{Terminal Stdout Sinks}: Unclipped shell outputs (\texttt{make} logs, compiler warnings) represented 67.8\% of tool payload tokens, demonstrating that automated output clipping is essential to prevent context dilution regardless of prompt architecture.
\end{enumerate}

Across all 89 tasks, evaluation expenditure and outcomes present a three-layer economic portrait. In absolute terms, Baseline incurred the lowest total evaluation cost under the recorded OpenRouter rates (\$27.33), followed by Superpowers (\$29.35) and Supreme (\$29.80). At the per-execution level, Baseline again recorded the lowest cost per attempted task under these rates (\$0.307), followed by Superpowers (\$0.330) and Supreme (\$0.335). These absolute and per-attempt cost advantages coincide with Baseline's lower token footprint (31.22M total billed tokens vs.\ 34.52M for Supreme), but also with its lower task completion (55/89 vs.\ 61/89).

Cost per successful task is a composite outcome-efficiency measure combining total evaluation expenditure with the number of tasks successfully completed; it is not an independent cost measurement. On this dimension, Supreme achieved the lowest effective evaluation cost per successful task under recorded OpenRouter rates (\$0.489), approximately 3.4\% below Superpowers (\$0.506), while completing three additional tasks (61 vs.\ 58). Baseline achieved \$0.497. The cost-per-successful-task ranking is unchanged under both list-price and recorded OpenRouter pricing as detailed in Table~\ref{tab:cost_economics}, confirming that this efficiency relationship is not an artifact of the specific pricing rates applied.

On the Hard task subset ($N=30$), the cost-per-successful-task ranking reverses: Baseline achieved the lowest cost per successful Hard task (\$0.645), followed by Supreme (\$0.680) and Superpowers (\$0.730). Supreme simultaneously achieved the highest Hard-task completion rate (66.7\% [20/30] vs.\ 56.7\% [17/30] for Baseline and 53.3\% [16/30] for Superpowers), representing a genuine trade-off of superior Hard-task coverage at moderately higher cost per successful Hard solve compared to Baseline.

\subsection{Practical Design Implications for Agent Systems}
The evaluated configurations suggest three practical design considerations:
\par\textbf{Static Invariant Principles}: Static placement of invariant engineering principles can avoid mid-turn documentation retrieval while maintaining consistent behavioral constraints across turns.
\par\textbf{Loop Termination Heuristics}: Explicit iteration thresholds and hypothesis-checking gates help limit prolonged exploratory scripting on open-ended combinatorial problems.
\par\textbf{Targeted Line Edits}: Surgical diff tools (\texttt{replace\_file\_content}) correlate with reduced collateral modifications compared to whole-file rewrites.

\subsection{Threats to Validity and Limitations}
\textbf{Single Foundation Model}: All evaluations used Gemini 3.6 Flash High. Model-specific reasoning and tool policies may interact with prompt architecture; findings may not generalize across model families without multi-model replication.
\par\textbf{Single Run per Task}: Each task was evaluated with one run per configuration at $\tau=0.0$ (267 container runs, $\approx 50$ compute hours). While $\tau=0.0$ minimizes sampling variance, multi-seed replication is needed to measure run-to-run stability.
\par\textbf{Coupled Structure and Content}: Supreme and Superpowers differ in structure (static vs.\ dynamic) and content (constitutional axioms vs.\ TDD workflows). Controlled ablations isolating delivery mechanism from content remain future work.
\par\textbf{Prompt Freezing}: The Supreme constitutional prompt was frozen prior to benchmark execution; no prompt adjustments were made based on benchmark task failures.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
In this work, we compared constitutional static grounding and dynamic progressive skill loading under a shared foundation model, evaluation harness, and benchmark corpus across all 89 tasks of Terminal-Bench 2.1 (267 container evaluations). As Iteration~1 of an ongoing evaluation program, these findings establish an initial empirical baseline for terminal agent dynamics; future iterations will expand to multi-model evaluations and controlled content-delivery ablations. Constitutional static grounding achieved a favorable position in task completion (61/89, 68.5\%), aggregate wall-clock time (15.15\,h, 17.2\% lower than Superpowers), and effective evaluation cost per successful task under recorded OpenRouter rates (\$0.489, 3.4\% below Superpowers), with a directional Hard-task advantage (66.7\% vs.\ 53.3\%; McNemar $p = 0.22$). While aggregate pass rates across all 89 tasks are not statistically distinguishable under paired McNemar tests ($p > 0.05$), and configurations differ in both structure and instructional content, the evaluation reveals substantial differences in execution dynamics, token economics, and behavioral trajectories across the analyzed cohorts. Cryptographic verification ledgers, per-run telemetry, and extraction dossiers are available to support independent verification of the reported aggregates.
